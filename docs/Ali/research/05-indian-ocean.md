## Why this region is a different variant

MENA's problem is **episodic**: a dry coast, a rare storm, a sudden pulse. The Western
Indian Ocean's problem is **chronic-plus-seasonal**: semi-arid catchments whose sediment
yield has been amplified by deforestation, agriculture and mining, delivering large
seasonal plumes onto reefs year after year.

**Why that distinction matters commercially [judgement]:** where sediment delivery is
chronic, the buyer wants *watershed management* (which agency programmes already provide),
not *event forecasting*. ReefShield's forecast mode is worth less. Its **exposure mapping
and satellite validation components are worth more**, because they answer "which reef is
being hit hardest and by whom."

## Site 13 · Toliara and Ranobe Bay, SW Madagascar — Score 10/12

**The most ecologically urgent site in this scan, and the poorest.**

| Criterion | Score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| C1 ephemeral | 1 | **Semi-arid** zone; Fiherenana and Onilahy are strongly seasonal rather than fully ephemeral **[sourced]** |
| C2 intense episodic | 2 | Cyclone-exposed coast with intense wet-season rainfall **[sourced]** |
| C3 reef proximity | 2 | **Le Grand Récif de Toliara**, part of one of the world's largest barrier reef systems (~400 km); Toliara Bay is bordered by the Fiherenana delta **[sourced]** |
| C4 confined basin | 2 | Ranobe Bay is a **semi-enclosed lagoon** encompassing two barrier reefs, two coral banks, three lagoon reefs and a fringing reef **[sourced]** |
| C5 development | 1 | Toliara city; the pressure is agricultural and mining, upstream |
| C6 data-poor | 2 | Very data-poor; NGO-led monitoring (Reef Doctor) rather than agency systems **[sourced]** |

### The evidence

- **High sedimentation results from poor management of mining and agricultural activities
  in the two main upstream rivers — Fiherenana to the north and Onilahy to the south — and
  this affects coral assemblages** **[sourced]**.
- **Sedimentation from upstream deforestation has the potential to smother corals,
  affecting the health of the entire reef ecosystem** **[sourced]**.
- The setting is striking: **spiny forest and baobabs within miles of one of the world's
  largest coral reef systems** **[sourced]**.

### What is different from Aqaba

**[judgement]**

- **Stakeholder is not a tourism authority.** The reef supports a subsistence and artisanal
  fishery and the communities are among the poorest in the world. The buyer is an NGO, a
  development bank, or a conservation programme — not a marine park with a monitoring
  budget.
- **The intervention point is upstream, not marine.** If you can attribute plume load to
  specific upstream land uses, that is directly actionable for reforestation and mining
  regulation. ReefShield's catchment attribution would be the valued component.
- **Funding logic is grant/impact, not subscription.** This is a *credibility and impact*
  site for the pitch's §27 impact section, not a revenue site.

## Site 14 · Malindi–Watamu and the Sabaki River, Kenya — Score 8/12

**The best-documented long-term sediment-to-reef case in the region — and a cautionary
tale about attribution.**

| Criterion | Score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| C1 ephemeral | 0 | Sabaki (Athi) and Tana are **perennial rivers** — this is the main disqualifier **[sourced]** |
| C2 intense episodic | 1 | Two monsoon rainy seasons; **turbidity plumes appear seasonally, during and immediately following the rains** **[sourced]** |
| C3 reef proximity | 2 | Malindi Marine National Park reefs sit directly in the plume's path **[sourced]** |
| C4 confined basin | 1 | Malindi Bay |
| C5 development | 2 | Rapid population growth and catchment land-use change **[sourced]** |
| C6 data-poor | 2 | Studied academically for decades; no operational system |

### Why it is scientifically valuable even though it scores lower

- **Since the early 1960s, increased soil erosion from changing land use in the Sabaki
  catchment has increased river-sediment discharge into coastal waters around Malindi**
  **[sourced]**.
- **The largest sediment pulse, from the April–May long rains, is transported north from
  the river mouths by prevailing southern monsoon winds and currents** **[sourced]** — i.e.
  plume direction is wind- and current-controlled, exactly the mechanism Nizar's particle
  engine models.
- **Limited southward plume movement during the NE monsoon has ensured the reef ecosystem
  in the northern parts of Malindi Marine National Park has not been completely destroyed**
  **[sourced]** — a natural experiment showing that *plume trajectory determines which reef
  survives*. That is the clearest external justification for the whole transport-modelling
  half of ReefShield **[judgement]**.
- East African soil erosion has been **recorded in a 300-year-old coral colony in Kenya**
  ([GRL](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006GL028525))
  **[sourced]** — corals archive terrestrial sediment history, which is a powerful
  validation avenue.

**[judgement]** Kenya fails C1 (perennial rivers) so it is **not** a deployment target, but
its literature is the strongest external support for the claim that *plume direction, not
just plume size, decides reef outcomes*. **Use it as evidence in the pitch, not as a market.**

## Somalia, Tanzania, Mozambique — briefly

**[judgement]** Somalia's arid coast fits the physical signature but is inaccessible.
Tanzania and Mozambique are wetter and dominated by perennial rivers, so they fall outside
the signature and into the same category as the Great Barrier Reef catchments — a
watershed-management problem with existing frameworks. Not pursued in this scan.
