## 1 · What problem are we solving?

It rains hard for a few hours in a desert. The water rushes down a dry valley — a *wadi* —
picking up sand, soil and dirt from roads and towns. All of it pours into the sea in one big
pulse.

Coral reefs sitting just offshore get buried in that muddy water. Corals need clean, clear water
to survive. A single flood can smother them.

**ReefShield predicts where that muddy water will go, before it gets there** — so the people who
manage the reef know which part to check first.

Right now nobody does this for desert coasts.

---

## 2 · Is this only an Aqaba problem?

No. That was the first thing we checked.

We scored **21 places around the world** against six simple tests. Does the valley only flow
after storms? Does the rain come in short violent bursts? Is there reef close to the river mouth?
Is the sea narrow enough to trap the mud? Is there a city or port at the mouth? And is the place
currently unmonitored?

**Six places in the Middle East score as high as Aqaba. Four of them sit on the same stretch of
water** — a gulf only 14 to 26 km wide, shared by Jordan, Israel, Egypt and Saudi Arabia.

**One storm floods all four countries within two days.** No single government can fix that alone,
which is exactly why shared regional funding makes sense here.

Worldwide, **more than a quarter of all coral reefs** are threatened by pollution washing off the
land.

→ [`09`](09-scorecard.md) has every site and its score

### One place we deliberately said no to

**Ningaloo Reef in Australia** looks like a perfect match — desert coast, reef right against the
shore. But its water quality is *excellent*, precisely because almost no water ever runs off the
land there.

We score it 6 out of 12 and say plainly that it is **not our problem to solve.**

That matters more than it sounds. Being able to explain why a famous desert reef *doesn't* need
us is what makes the whole screening method believable. → [`01`](01-signature.md)

## 3 · Has someone already built this?

Partly — and knowing exactly who is one of the most useful things in this research.

**Australia built a system called eReefs** for the Great Barrier Reef. It works, it runs every
day, and it does roughly what we want to do. It also took **four national agencies and about
AU$26 million**, and it assumes rivers that flow all year round with measuring stations along
them.

The Gulf of Aqaba has **27 mm of rain a year, no permanent rivers, and no measuring stations.**
eReefs has nothing to feed on here.

**NOAA** in the United States built a water-clarity product for reefs after heavy rain. It covers
exactly **two places on Earth** — Hawaii and Puerto Rico — and it is still officially
experimental. It also only watches what is already happening; it does not predict.

So: **every individual piece of this exists somewhere. The combination does not exist for any
desert coast.**

**What we can honestly claim:** the integration, on a data-poor arid coast, from free satellite
data alone.

**What we must never claim:** that nobody has modelled sediment or reefs before. That is false and
a judge will know. → [`08`](08-prior-art.md)

## 4 · Who would pay for it?

This is where the strongest finding in the whole set sits.

### There is already money set aside for exactly this problem

The UN Development Programme runs a **US$10 million programme for Gulf of Aqaba reefs**, running
until 2030. **The Aqaba Marine Reserve is one of its priority sites.**

Their own funding document lists as a key threat:

> *"changed rainfall patterns / intensity that could increase flows of freshwater, sediments and
> pollution onto coral reefs"*

That is our problem, in their words, in a signed document. And the programme funds **"water
quality / land-ocean interface projects"** as one of the things it pays for.

There is also a **second, separate project already running** at the same reserve: US$663,000 from
the Global Environment Facility plus US$5.5 million in co-financing, with **UNDP Jordan
executing** it.

**So this is not a cold pitch.** UNDP Jordan, IUCN and the Government of Jordan have already
signed documents saying this problem matters. We do not have to convince them it is real — we
have to show them we built the tool.

**One thing nobody has checked:** whether that US$10 million proposal actually got funded. It was
signed in late 2022. **Somebody should find out**, because it decides whether this is a live
opportunity or old news. → [`14`](14-funding.md)

### Who signs the cheque

| Who | What they'd pay | What they are |
|---|---|---|
| **ASEZA** — Aqaba's zone authority | $40–120k/year | The main customer. But **charge nothing in year one** and let a grant cover it |
| **NEOM / Red Sea Global** | $150–400k/year | Where the real money is. Building right now, on the same gulf |
| **UNDP, IUCN, GEF** | Pays for the first deployments | Not customers — the ones who fund the start |
| **Dive centres** | $10–30k/year as a group | Users, not really buyers. **Give them it free** — thirty operators who rely on it daily is the best argument for renewal |

### What they are actually buying

Not a computer model. Three practical things:

1. **Where to send the survey team tomorrow morning.** They have a handful of staff and 27 km of
   coast.
2. **A record they can show their government and their donors** — what was predicted, what was
   observed, timestamped.
3. **Cover when something goes wrong.** *"We had no way of knowing"* is a weak position.
   *"Our system flagged it, we sampled within 12 hours, here's the data"* is a strong one.

**And here is the timing insight that matters most:** organisations like this **buy after a
disaster, not before one.** So the system has to already be running, and already working, when
the next flood arrives. That argues for getting something live and cheap now, not for a polished
sales pitch later. → [`12`](12-buyers.md)

## 5 · Is it a real business?

Yes, but a modest one — and being honest about that is an advantage.

**The market is small: about 12 places we could realistically serve.** Not hundreds.

**Running it is very cheap.** All the satellite and weather data we use is free. Servers cost
roughly **$140–480 per site per month** — around **2–7%** of what we'd charge. Money is not the
constraint.

**The realistic picture:** roughly **$45,000** profit per site in year one, **$73,000** per site
per year after that. **Six sites would bring in about $440,000 a year** — enough to support a
team of three or four people.

**That is not a venture-capital business, and we should not pretend it is.** It is closer to a
specialist consultancy with a software product. Saying so in front of judges who have just heard
ten inflated market claims is a real differentiator.

### The one thing that limits growth

It is not the software. **It is proving the system works at each new place.**

Every new coast needs its own documented flood and its own careful expert review of satellite
images. That review cannot be automated yet, so **the cost does not drop as we grow.**

Moving to a new coast takes about **15–25 days of work, and 60–70% of that is the proving, not
the coding.**

**The fix, long term:** save every reviewed image properly from day one. At around 50–100 of
them, the review can be automated, and the economics change completely. → [`13`](13-economics.md)

### Two numbers to be careful with

**Don't overstate what the reef is worth.** Published estimates disagree by a factor of a hundred.
Use the conservative one. It gives:

- Aqaba Marine Reserve: **$0.25–1.5 million a year**
- All of Jordan's reef: **$0.6–3.8 million a year**

The strong version of the argument is simple: **a system costing tens of thousands protects
something worth millions — and unlike the reef, the system can be replaced.**

The tempting version uses the highest published figure and gets you "$200 million of reef." It
falls apart under one question, and it drags every honest number down with it. **Don't.**
→ [`11`](11-market.md)

## 6 · What have we actually built?

Being straight about this matters, because the concept document shows an architecture diagram
where **most of the boxes don't exist yet.**

**What works, and works well:**

| Part | Who | State |
|---|---|---|
| Rainfall and weather data | Karam | ✅ Done |
| Land, soil, reef and depth maps | Pulga | ✅ Done |
| Terrain, catchments and river outlets | Mahdi | ✅ Done |
| Satellite plume detection | Abd | ⚠️ Started — **and the answer came back bad, see §7** |
| Weather forecasts and ocean currents | Nizar | ⚠️ Started — no simulation engine yet |
| **The actual product — website, database, API** | — | ❌ **Nothing exists** |

**5,424 lines** of working code. **258 tests.**

**The awkward part:** the finished work is the hardest to fake and the least impressive to look
at. The missing work is what everyone wants to see in a demo. That is a presentation problem as
much as an engineering one — **show what runs, and clearly mark the rest as roadmap.**

**What is genuinely impressive** and worth pointing out:

- The code **refuses to guess.** Where the weather data format is ambiguous, it proves what the
  numbers mean and stops with an error rather than assuming.
- **Missing data is never quietly turned into zero** — a very common and very damaging shortcut.
- Placeholder data is **labelled as placeholder inside the data itself.**
- **A past mistake was turned into a permanent test.** An area that was once wrongly too small is
  now literally named `RETIRED_BOX` in the code, with three tests making sure it never comes back.

**What is missing and easy to fix:** there is still no list of software dependencies, which means
**nobody has ever actually run the test suite on this machine.** That is about an hour of work,
and it is the cheapest way to look more credible. → [`16`](16-build-state.md) · [`17`](17-scaling.md)

## 7 · What is going wrong right now

Two things you need to know.

### The satellite validation plan failed

The plan was to prove the system works by finding a satellite photo of the October 2016 mud plume
and comparing it to what the model predicts.

**There is no such photo.** The team checked properly and wrote it up in
[`docs/event_audit.md`](../event_audit.md).

The nearest clear satellite pass was **five days after the flood** — and by then the muddy water
had **already dispersed and vanished, two to three days earlier.** Two different satellites
confirm it. The backup event in 2013 is worse: the satellites we'd need either weren't launched
yet or weren't working.

**This is not bad luck with clouds or a data problem.** The mud simply clears faster than
satellites come around. No amount of searching fixes it.

**The way forward, and it is genuinely good news:** there is a **measuring instrument** that sat
250 m offshore through the whole 2016 flood, recording saltiness and muddiness **every five
minutes.** Those numbers are published.

So instead of comparing our model to a picture, **we compare it to actual measurements** — a
salinity drop of 1.75 units and a muddiness peak of 2.18 g/L, at a known place and a known time.

**That is a stronger test than a photo ever was.** But it needs the water-movement simulation
built, and that does not exist yet. **This is now the most urgent thing in the project.**

### The credentials are still exposed

Team passwords for four data services were committed into the project's history. The file has
since been deleted — **but deleting a file does not remove it from the history.** Anyone who
copies the repository can still read them.

**They need to be changed. That has not happened.** A customer's security check would find this
immediately. → [`18`](18-risks.md)

## 9 · Things we could not verify — do not use these

The research is deliberately explicit about its own gaps. **Keep it that way.** One made-up number
discredits all the real ones.

| Claim | Why not |
|---|---|
| "About 28,000 km of desert coastline worldwide" | **We could not find this in the paper it was credited to.** The other possible source is paywalled. **Do not use** |
| "$6.91 billion unlocked for ocean projects" | Came from the hackathon briefing. **We could not confirm it independently** |
| Aqaba Marine Reserve visitor numbers and budget | We got the area (2.45 km²), the coastline (7 km) and the dive sites (19). **The budget and visitor numbers are still unread** — the PDF blocks automated downloads. **This is the most valuable missing number in the business case** |
| Whether the US$10 M programme is still active | Signed late 2022. **Nobody has checked its current status** |

**How to read the labels in these documents:**

- **[sourced]** — it says so in a document we cite. Follow the link.
- **[derived]** — we calculated it, and the working is shown.
- **[judgement]** — our opinion. Arguable.
- **[assumption]** — a business estimate with no public comparison. Arithmetic shown so you can
  replace it.
- **[unverified]** — **do not cite this.**
